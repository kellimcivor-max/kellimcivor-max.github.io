Unifying engineering knowledge across product development

The teams

Product organizations building intelligent machines

John Deere's Intelligence Solutions Group (ISG) and Ag & Turf teams build the embedded software, electrical, and hardware systems that go directly into machines. Narsi's group leads AI enablement for product development; Ryan's group leads AI for embedded software on vehicle products. These are product organizations — not global IT or manufacturing — developing electromechanical systems where software runs on hardware that sits on the machine.

The goal is a durable, agentic product-development workflow spanning the people who build these systems: software engineers, electrical engineers, product managers, and customer support engineers.

The challenge

Engineering knowledge is scattered across too many systems

Design intent, risk assessments, technical specifications, hydraulic and electrical schematics, requirements, code repositories, tickets, and tribal knowledge all live in separate places. Before a team can act — design a part, trace a change, or answer a question — they must first reconstruct the history, ownership, and downstream impact of the work.

Where engineering knowledge lives today

Three compounding issues

Why the fragmentation hurts

Weak workflow durability

Knowledge fragmented across software and hardware teams means workflows break and context is lost. There is no reusable layer that keeps product knowledge continuous as the team develops.

No cross-system context

A single change can ripple across the system. Today there is no way to see and reason across the relationships between schematics, shared components, and controllers in one place.

Unstructured, siloed knowledge

Critical information lives in spreadsheets on shared drives, in individual engineers' heads, and in documents that can't be structured — with no way to bring it together.

A concrete example

One component change touches everything

Multiple machine platforms use different hydraulic schematics but share the same pressure sensor. That sensor is used across multiple controllers, each with its own electrical schematic, all connected in a network. There is no single place to trace those relationships — so understanding the impact of one change means manually piecing it together across systems.

Illustrative relationship map: a shared sensor links platforms, controllers, and schematics that live in separate systems today.
Why existing tools fall short

Today's AI tools don't close the gap

The team relies on GitHub Copilot as its predominant AI tool and has access to other foundation models, including Claude. Neither reaches across the systems where engineering knowledge lives.

GitHub Copilot Predominant tool

  • Only a handful of connectors into enterprise systems.
  • Lacks the context to reach across John Deere's data.
  • Answers come back generic — hard to fold into engineering work.

Claude & standalone models In use

  • Not secure across the enterprise.
  • Cannot safely span John Deere's internal systems.
  • Everyone "navigating their own waters" — not cohesive, introduces security risk.

Reach across enterprise systems

The gap isn't model quality — it's how much of the enterprise each tool can securely see and connect.

The desired outcome

A secure context & connectivity layer beneath existing tools

Rather than replacing Copilot or GitHub, the goal is a layer that unifies knowledge underneath them — feeding secure, connected context into where the team already works.

Scope & next step

The team wants to first validate whether the context connection makes sense for a focused group — Narsi, Ryan, and a few team members — before evaluating enterprise-scale governance and rollout.
